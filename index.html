Does Your Degree Decide Your Destiny?

Every year, tens of thousands of Australians graduate with a degree and wonder the same question:
what happens next?

This visualisation follows Australian graduates from Enrollment into the workforce, comparing eight broad fields of study on employment, further study, pay and unemployment.

What Do People Study?

Business and management, society and culture and health have drawn the most students since 2015. Their appeal is breadth: each leads to a wide range of careers, so Enrollments alone don't show where graduates end up.

The growth is elsewhere. Information technology went from the smallest field in 2015 to the fourth largest in 2024, including a +37.7% jump in 2023.

Outside events matter too. Between 2019 and 2021, business and management commencements fell 23% while health grew 9%. Do these shifts lead to better outcomes? Follow the graduates to find out.

What Happens After Graduation?

Where graduates end up depends heavily on their field. In 2025, education graduates were the most likely to move straight into professional full-time work (72.4%), at a time of a national shortage of teachers.

Natural and physical sciences graduates took a different route. At 35.9%, they had the highest share going on to further study, meaning fewer entered the workforce directly.

For some graduates, neither path opened up as easily. Creative arts (29.4%) and information technology (22.7%) had the largest shares outside both full-time work and further study, pointing to a harder entry into those industries.

It does get easier. Three years after graduating, every field has a higher share of the 2022 cohort in professional full-time work. The biggest gains went to the fields that started weakest: creative arts climbed from 36.1% to 70.9%, and natural and physical sciences and society and culture each gained about 30%.

Information technology shows the sharpest turnaround. After a difficult start for many graduates, it reached 90.5%, ahead of engineering and second only to education. Education started highest and gained least, so the gap between fields has narrowed from about 44% in 2022 to 22% in 2025.

Slow starts don't have to be the end of the story.
But a professional job isn't the same as a well-paid one, so what is each field worth?

What Is It Worth?

A professional job and a good salary tend to go together, but not always. Education (72.4%) and engineering (70.6%) lead on professional full-time work, and both pay over $82,000. Health is the exception at the top. It has the highest median salary ($85,375) but only 56.3% in professional full-time roles.

Creative arts sits at the bottom on both measures, with 27.0% and $63,200, about $22,000 less than health.

Size doesn't guarantee pay either: business and management and society and culture, the two biggest fields by enrolment, both earn under $76,000.

But a salary at one point in time is only part of the picture. How quickly does pay grow?

Within three years, the median salary of the 2022 graduates rose in every field, by at least $17,800. Engineering gained the most (+$32,000, to $103,000), followed by information technology (+$30,900) and business and management (+$30,000), which climbed from seventh to fourth place.

Education went the other way. It started with the second-highest salary ($72,300) but grew the least in percentage terms (+28.1%), and finished fifth. Health kept its lead at $105,250.

Within fields, the pay gap by gender is small but mostly points one way: men's median salary is higher in seven of the eight fields. Natural and physical sciences has the largest gap ($5,200), followed by society and culture ($3,300) and education ($3,100). Information technology is the closest to parity, at $800.

Creative arts is the one reversal, with women earning $1,700 more than men, though from the lowest salaries of any field.

How Has It Changed?

Pay rankings have barely changed. Health has had the highest median graduate salary every year since 2015, and creative arts has had the lowest, with no exceptions in ten years.

The movement is in the middle. Engineering overtook education for second place in 2023, as it had briefly in 2017. Natural and physical sciences moved the most. It reached fourth in 2018 but had fallen to seventh by 2023, behind society and culture since 2022.

Finding full-time work got harder for most fields between 2022 and 2025. The share of graduates not in full-time work rose in seven of the eight fields, with information technology experiencing the biggest jump, up 11.3 points to 34.7%.

Education (+0.5 points) and health (+1.0) barely moved. Natural and physical sciences was the only field to improve (-1.3 points), but its 37.6% is mostly graduates in further study (35.9%), not graduates looking for work.
